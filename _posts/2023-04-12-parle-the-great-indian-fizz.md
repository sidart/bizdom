In 1939, the Chauhans' one-oven biscuit factory at Vile Parle began making Gluco biscuits. Six months later came Monaco, named after the European principality by Maneklal Chauhan, whom the family called ‘Mr Englishman’. In 1949, his brother Jayantilal ventured into soft drinks.

Parle's first drink, Gluco-Cola, had an advertising campaign designed by a young R.K. Laxman. Coca-Cola, which began bottling in India the following year, objected to the name. According to Ramesh Chauhan, Coke brought in British Queen's Counsels to fight the case. Unable to sustain the legal expenses, the Chauhans renamed their drink Parle Cola, which soon disappeared.

Gold Spot, launched in the early 1950s, derived its name from an existing Parle peppermint called Gold Star. One of its advertising films, directed by Shyam Benegal, featured a young Rekha drinking straight from the bottle. In 1983, Parle ran a promotion with Walt Disney, wherein children collected Gold Spot bottle caps bearing Jungle Book characters to obtain a special comic.

Limca (1971) owed its cloudy appearance to the difficulty of procuring green glass bottles. The company used a technique called microencapsulation to distinguish its lemonade; early tasters thought it resembled soap water. The name came from the Marathi *limbu cha*, though the advertising agency preferred Parle Lime 'n' Lemon.

In 1976 came Maaza, offering mango pulp in a bottled, non-carbonated drink. Following Coca-Cola's exit in 1977, Ramesh Chauhan experimented with cinnamon, cardamom, nutmeg and citrus oils while developing Thums Up. Its original advertising jingle was ‘Happy days are here again’. The association with adventure and masculinity came much later.

By the time Pepsi arrived, only four of Parle's 62 bottling plants were company-owned. The rest were franchisees, some of whom began switching to Pepsi.

On 12 November 1993, Parle assigned the trademarks of Thums Up, Limca, Maaza, Gold Spot, Citra and Rim Zim to Coca-Cola. The transaction, however, did not cover all overseas rights to Maaza. In 2008, almost six decades after the Gluco-Cola episode, the Chauhans and Coca-Cola were back in a trademark dispute.
