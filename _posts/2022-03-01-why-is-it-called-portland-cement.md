Portland cement, the most widely used type of cement in the world, has nothing to do with Portland, the largest city in the American state of Oregon. Its name comes from the Isle of Portland in Dorset, England, known for its pale-coloured limestone.

The stone had been a favourite amongst British architects for centuries. Sir Christopher Wren used it extensively while rebuilding St. Paul's Cathedral following the Great Fire of London in 1666.

On 21 October 1824, Joseph Aspdin, a bricklayer from Leeds, obtained British patent No. 5022 for a method of manufacturing artificial stone. His process involved heating a mixture of limestone and clay and grinding it into a fine powder. Since the hardened material resembled Portland stone, he named it Portland cement.

Aspdin's son William subsequently improved the manufacturing process by employing higher kiln temperatures, producing a material closer to the cement we use today.

Incidentally, India's early experiment with cement manufacturing began in Madras in 1904, using seashells as a source of lime. The venture was commercially unsuccessful.

A decade later, the Indian Cement Company established a factory at Porbandar in Gujarat, marking the beginning of large-scale cement manufacturing in India. Tata Sons was its managing agent.
