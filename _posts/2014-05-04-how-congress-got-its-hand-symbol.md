During India's first general elections in 1951–52, the election symbol of the Indian National Congress was a pair of yoked bullocks. With the country's literacy rate at around 18 per cent, the Election Commission had decided to allot political parties symbols drawn from everyday Indian life, which voters could easily recognise.

The first change in the Congress symbol came following the split in the party in 1969. With both factions laying claim to the original symbol, neither was eventually permitted to use it. The faction led by S. Nijalingappa, known as Congress (O), was allotted a woman spinning a charkha, while the faction backed by Indira Gandhi, Congress (R), received the cow and calf. Indira went on to win the 1971 general elections with this symbol.

Over the following years, with Sanjay Gandhi's growing influence in the party, the cow and calf became the subject of political satire. Opposition politicians began drawing parallels between the two animals and the mother-son duo.

Following the electoral defeat of 1977, Congress underwent another split in January 1978. Indira Gandhi's faction, subsequently known as Congress (I), needed a new election symbol.

Political journalist Rasheed Kidwai recounts an amusing episode in his book *24 Akbar Road*. Buta Singh, then the AICC general secretary, had been given three choices by the Election Commission: an elephant, a bicycle and an open palm.

Indira was in Vijayawada with P.V. Narasimha Rao when Singh booked a trunk call to seek her approval. Owing to the poor telephone connection, Indira repeatedly mistook *haath* (hand) for *haathi* (elephant) and kept rejecting the suggestion.

An exasperated Indira eventually handed the telephone to Rao. Realising the source of the confusion, Rao asked Singh to use the word *panja* instead of *haath*. Indira immediately approved the choice.

On 2 February 1978, the Election Commission recognised Congress (I) as a national party and allotted it the hand symbol.

Incidentally, Congress was not the first political party in independent India to use the hand. During the first general elections, a version of the symbol had been allotted to the All India Forward Bloc (Ruikar Group).
