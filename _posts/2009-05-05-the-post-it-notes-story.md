In 1968, Spencer Silver, a scientist working at the American manufacturing company 3M, developed an unusual adhesive. Unlike conventional adhesives, it allowed objects to be attached to a surface and subsequently removed without leaving much residue. However, for several years, Silver struggled to find a commercial application for his invention.

One of his colleagues, Arthur Fry, sang in a church choir and was irritated by the paper bookmarks that kept falling out of his hymn book. Fry made use of 3M’s famed 15 per cent rule, which allowed employees to devote a portion of their working hours to projects of their own choosing, to develop a reusable bookmark using Silver’s adhesive.

Working with colleagues from the engineering and production departments, Fry developed a process for applying the adhesive to small pieces of paper. During internal product trials, employees began using them to attach handwritten comments to documents. Fry realised that his invention had greater commercial potential as an office stationery product.

In 1977, 3M introduced the product in four American cities under the name Press ’n Peel, with disappointing results. The company subsequently distributed free samples to office workers in Boise, Idaho, an exercise popularly known as the ‘Boise Blitz’.

The response encouraged 3M to launch Post-it Notes across the United States in April 1980.

Incidentally, the product’s familiar canary-yellow colour was reportedly an accident. The laboratory next door had some yellow scrap paper, which Fry used during the initial experiments.
