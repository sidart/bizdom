In January 1975, the American electronics magazine *Popular Electronics* featured a rather unusual machine on its cover. Called the Altair 8800, it was a computer that could be assembled at home and was available as a kit for $395. It had neither a keyboard nor a display, and users had to operate it through a series of switches on its front panel.

The man behind the machine was Henry Edward Roberts, an electronics engineer who had established Micro Instrumentation and Telemetry Systems (MITS) in Albuquerque, New Mexico, in 1969. MITS initially manufactured electronic kits for model rocket enthusiasts and subsequently ventured into the electronic calculator business.

However, the entry of large semiconductor manufacturers into the calculator market brought down prices considerably, leaving smaller manufacturers such as MITS struggling with debt. Roberts decided to develop an affordable computer using Intel's recently introduced 8080 microprocessor. This was how the Altair 8800 came into being.

The magazine cover caught the attention of Paul Allen, who showed it to his childhood friend Bill Gates, then a student at Harvard University. The duo contacted Roberts, offering to develop a version of the BASIC programming language for his computer. At the time, they had neither access to an Altair nor a working version of the software they were proposing to sell.

Allen subsequently demonstrated the program at MITS. It worked, and the resulting software, Altair BASIC, became the first commercial product of the business that Gates and Allen established in 1975. They called it Micro-Soft, combining the words microcomputer and software.

Albuquerque, rather than Seattle, was thus the original home of Microsoft.

Roberts sold MITS to Pertec Computer Corporation in 1977 and moved to Georgia, where he took up farming. He subsequently returned to university to pursue medicine, becoming a qualified doctor in 1986 and practising in the small town of Cochran.

On 1 April 2010, Roberts died at the age of 68 following a prolonged illness. In a joint statement, Gates and Allen recalled how Roberts had been willing to take a chance on two young men interested in computers at a time when few people owned one.

By the time of his death, the man often referred to as the father of the personal computer had spent more years practising medicine than running a computer company.
