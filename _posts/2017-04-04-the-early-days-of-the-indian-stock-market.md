Much of the early stockbroking business in Bombay was an extension of the city's mercantile activities. Shares of banks and cotton presses were among the earliest securities to be traded. The American Civil War (1861–65), which disrupted the supply of American cotton to Europe, brought considerable wealth to Bombay's cotton merchants. Some of this money found its way into the stock market, resulting in one of India's earliest speculative bubbles.

In 1875, the brokers organised themselves under the Native Share and Stock Brokers' Association, which eventually became the Bombay Stock Exchange (BSE).

In those days, broking firms were generally family-run enterprises and were known by the names of their founders. Among the prominent names were D.S. Purbhoodas & Co. (DSP), Jamnadas Morarjee, Champaklal Devidas (which later became Cifco Finance) and Brijmohan Laxminarayan (established in 1915). When Hemendra Kothari joined D.S. Purbhoodas in 1969, he represented the fourth generation of his family in the stockbroking business.

Bombay's early securities business had a considerable presence of Parsis and, over time, Gujarati trading families became increasingly prominent. In eastern India, Marwari merchants who had migrated from Rajasthan built a substantial presence in the broking business. The Calcutta Stock Exchange, formally established in 1908, grew around the trading of shares in jute, tea and coal companies. Ahmedabad, with its numerous textile mills, had established its own stock exchange in 1894.

Much before computer terminals appeared on Dalal Street, trading was conducted through the open outcry system. There were jobbers who specialised in particular shares, quoting buying and selling prices and earning money on the difference. Another feature of the Indian stock market was *badla*, an arrangement under which speculators could carry forward their outstanding positions to the next settlement period by paying a financing charge.

The introduction of screen-based trading by the National Stock Exchange in 1994, followed by BSE in 1995, gradually did away with the need for brokers to be physically present in the trading ring. For generations of Bombay's broking families, the membership card of the stock exchange had been a valuable family asset in its own right.
