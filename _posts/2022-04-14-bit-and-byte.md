In July 1948, Claude Shannon, an American mathematician working at Bell Laboratories, published a paper titled *A Mathematical Theory of Communication*. The paper contained what is generally regarded as the first published use of the word ‘bit’ in computing and information theory.

Shannon, often referred to as the father of information theory, credited his colleague John W. Tukey with suggesting the word, a contraction of ‘binary digit’. Incidentally, Tukey is also credited with popularising the term ‘software’ through a paper published in 1958.

The word ‘byte’ came into existence in June 1956, when Werner Buchholz, an engineer at IBM, was working on an experimental supercomputer called Stretch. He deliberately changed the spelling of ‘bite’ to ‘byte’ to avoid its accidental confusion with ‘bit’.

Initially, a byte could contain anywhere between one and six bits. It was IBM's System/360 family of computers, launched in April 1964, that helped establish the eight-bit byte as an industry standard.

Shannon himself had some rather unusual interests outside information theory, including juggling, unicycling and gambling. In 1961, along with mathematician Edward O. Thorp, he developed what is generally regarded as the world's first wearable computer.

Approximately the size of a cigarette packet, the device was operated through switches concealed inside a shoe and was designed to predict where a roulette ball would land. The two tested their invention in Las Vegas, although problems with its wiring prevented sustained use.

Thorp subsequently applied his mathematical abilities to the stock market and became one of the early practitioners of quantitative investing.
