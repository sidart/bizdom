In October 1999, a relatively young telecommunications equipment company called Sycamore Networks made its debut on Nasdaq. Within a few months, its market capitalisation touched $55 billion, making its co-founder, Gururaj Deshpande, popularly known as Desh, one of the wealthiest Indians in America.

When Shivanand Kanavi interviewed Deshpande for *Business India* in early 2001, Sycamore was already feeling the effects of the technology stock market correction. Its market capitalisation had fallen to around $15 billion, although it was still trading at a price-to-earnings multiple of approximately 450.

Nearly three decades earlier, Deshpande had been contemplating a job with TELCO (now Tata Motors) in Pune, which would have paid him ₹500 a month.

### The Karnataka connection

Deshpande came from Hubli (now Hubballi), where his father worked for the government's labour department. His early education took him to several smaller towns of Karnataka before he joined National College in Bangalore and subsequently IIT Madras, where he studied electrical engineering.

After graduating in 1973, he received a scholarship to pursue his master's degree at the University of New Brunswick in Canada. His research involved microwaves and waveguides, structures used to transmit electromagnetic energy. Optical fibres, which would subsequently become central to his business interests, operate on related principles.

Deshpande initially intended to pursue an academic career and obtained his doctorate in data communications from Queen's University in Kingston, Canada. His transition into industry came through his association with Codex Corporation, a telecommunications equipment manufacturer that had become part of Motorola.

At Codex, he acquired experience in the commercial aspects of manufacturing communications equipment, including product development, customer acquisition and managing engineering teams. In 1984, he moved to the United States, choosing Boston over California.

Boston and its surrounding towns had developed their own technology industry around institutions such as MIT. The Route 128 highway, which encircles Boston, had become almost synonymous with the region's electronics and computer industry. Companies such as Digital Equipment Corporation and Wang Laboratories had their origins in this region.

Deshpande would establish his subsequent businesses in the same geographical neighbourhood.

### The early ventures

The emergence of local area networks (LANs) during the 1980s created a market for equipment that could connect computers within offices and other commercial establishments. One of the technologies under development was the Fibre Distributed Data Interface (FDDI), which used optical fibre to transmit data at speeds considerably higher than those possible on many existing networks.

Deshpande's first attempt at building a business around this technology ended following the stock market crash of October 1987, when raising capital became difficult for his fledgling enterprise.

He subsequently co-founded Coral Networks in 1988. The company was developing high-speed networking equipment, particularly routers, which direct data between computer networks.

In his conversation with Kanavi, Deshpande attributed his departure from Coral to a disagreement over when the company's product should be introduced into the market. He favoured selling equipment that could already offer a substantial improvement over existing products, while continuing research on faster versions. His partner preferred waiting until the more ambitious technical specifications could be achieved.

Deshpande left Coral in 1990.

### Cascade Communications

His next venture coincided with the early commercial development of the internet. Until then, much of the world's telecommunications infrastructure had been designed for voice traffic. As businesses began connecting their computers, telecom companies needed equipment that could handle growing volumes of data.

Traditional telephone networks established a dedicated circuit between two users for the duration of a call. Computer communications, on the other hand, involved intermittent bursts of data. A more economical way of handling this traffic was to divide it into packets and allow several users to share the same communications infrastructure.

Cascade Communications, established in 1990, developed equipment for this emerging market.

Among its products were switches based on Frame Relay technology, which enabled telecom operators to offer data communications services to corporate customers without having to provide each customer with dedicated physical infrastructure.

The business grew rapidly as American corporations connected their offices and computer networks. Cascade went public in 1994, and in 1997, Ascend Communications acquired the company for $3.7 billion.

Two years later, Lucent Technologies announced the acquisition of Ascend in a transaction valued at approximately $20 billion.

The acquisition was part of a wider consolidation within the American telecommunications equipment industry. Companies traditionally associated with telephone networks were paying considerable sums to acquire businesses that had developed expertise in transporting internet traffic.

### The Sycamore tree

In 1998, Deshpande established Sycamore Networks with optical communications specialists Eric Swanson and Richard Barry. Several former Cascade employees joined the new enterprise.

The economics of telecommunications was changing once again. Operators were laying extensive fibre-optic networks, and technologies such as wavelength-division multiplexing made it possible to transmit several independent streams of information through a single strand of optical fibre.

Sycamore developed equipment that could help telecom operators manage and provision this optical bandwidth more efficiently.

In October 1999, the company went public. During the subsequent months, investors valued Sycamore on the assumption that the demand for internet bandwidth would continue growing rapidly, requiring enormous investments in telecommunications equipment.

The origin of the company's name is worth mentioning. Deshpande told Kanavi that he preferred a name that would not restrict the company's future business interests to a particular technology. He was also attracted by the longevity associated with the Sycamore tree.

By the time Kanavi met him in Boston, Deshpande was speaking about building an institution that would endure for several generations.

Sycamore would survive for only about fifteen years.

The collapse of the technology bubble was followed by bankruptcies and a prolonged reduction in capital expenditure by telecom operators. Many of the optical networks constructed during the boom had considerably more capacity than their owners could commercially utilise.

In October 2012, Sycamore announced that it would sell substantially all the assets of its intelligent bandwidth management business for $18.75 million to an affiliate of Marlin Equity Partners. The company also announced its intention to liquidate and dissolve following the transaction.

### Back to India

Around the time Sycamore was attracting attention on Wall Street, another telecommunications equipment company was taking shape in Bangalore.

Established in 2000, Tejas Networks began developing optical networking products for telecom operators. One of its early assignments was for Tata Power's metropolitan optical network in Mumbai. It subsequently supplied equipment to Indian telecom companies, electricity utilities and RailTel.

Deshpande became an investor and chairman of the company, which went public in India in 2017.

He and his wife, Jaishree, had also become involved in funding scientific research and entrepreneurship. In 2002, they donated $20 million to establish the Deshpande Center for Technological Innovation at MIT. In 2007, their foundation started an initiative in Hubballi to encourage entrepreneurship in northern Karnataka.

In January 2017, the couple, along with Infosys co-founder Kris Gopalakrishnan, helped establish the Gopalakrishnan-Deshpande Centre for Innovation and Entrepreneurship at IIT Madras.

Twenty years have passed since the extraordinary stock market valuations of optical networking companies. This year, with offices, educational institutions and several other commercial establishments operating remotely on account of the coronavirus pandemic, the demand for telecommunications bandwidth has once again become a subject of considerable commercial interest.

For Deshpande, whose businesses were once dependent on the expenditure of American telecom operators, there is now an Indian connection to that demand. Among the applications of Tejas Networks' optical equipment is BharatNet, the government's programme to extend broadband connectivity to India's gram panchayats.
