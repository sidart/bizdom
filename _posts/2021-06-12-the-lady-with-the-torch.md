In 1918, brothers Harry and Jack Cohn, along with Joe Brandt, established a film production business in America called the Cohn-Brandt-Cohn Film Sales Corporation. Initially associated with low-budget films, the company was renamed Columbia Pictures in 1924.

One of its early logos featured a woman dressed as a Roman soldier, carrying a shield and a sheaf of wheat. The figure represented Columbia, a female personification of America, whose name was derived from that of Christopher Columbus. Columbia frequently appeared in American political cartoons, patriotic illustrations and recruitment posters.

Over the following decades, the film studio's logo underwent several changes. The shield and wheat eventually gave way to a torch, making the figure somewhat reminiscent of the Statue of Liberty. Actress Evelyn Venable is often credited as one of the early models for the Columbia Torch Lady.

The version familiar to moviegoers was created in 1992 by American artist Michael Deas. For the painting, photographer Kathy Anderson persuaded a colleague, Jenny Joseph, to pose wearing a makeshift drape and holding a small lamp. Joseph was a graphic artist at *The Times-Picayune*, a newspaper published in New Orleans, and had no previous experience as a professional model.

The painting was subsequently animated for Columbia's opening sequence. Joseph's brief modelling assignment thus gave Hollywood one of its most recognisable studio logos.
